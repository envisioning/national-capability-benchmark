## What Sweden built that no indicator counts

These are documented institutional changes in Sweden that the framework records as evidence. They appear beside the score and do not change it or its confidence.

- **BankID federated electronic identity** (Adaptability). Sweden's bank-issued BankID became a shared identity layer for public and private services: in 2024 it handled 7.6 billion identification or signature transactions for 8.6 million users across 7,500 connected services, with 99.99% availability.
- **Swish mobile instant payments** (Coordination). Sweden's six-bank-owned Getswish consortium built Swish into a national mobile payment rail: in 2023 it served just over 8.7 million private users and about 335,000 merchant or business users, processing approximately 977 million payments worth SEK 480 billion.
- **1177 national health portal and helpline** (Adaptability). Sweden's regions and municipalities run the 1177 national health hub through Inera, combining a common website, logged-in care services and the nationwide nurse helpline; 1177.se recorded just over 260 million visits in 2021.
- **VMA multi-channel public warning system** (Adaptability). Sweden's Viktigt meddelande till allmänheten (VMA) system combines radio, television, mobile messages, apps and outdoor sirens; SOS Alarm's 2025 report records 37 VMA alerts issued in 2024, alongside the four scheduled annual siren tests.
- **Nya Karolinska Solna public–private hospital project** (Building). Stockholm delivered Nya Karolinska Solna through a 30-year public–private partnership: the official inquiry reports SEK 22.8 billion for construction and equipment, a projected SEK 57 billion total through 2040 including operation and maintenance, and an external comparison that found construction 53% more expensive per square metre than Scandinavian hospitals.
- **Barsebäck nuclear plant closure and decommissioning** (Building). Sweden permanently shut Barsebäck 1 in 1999 and Barsebäck 2 in 2005; the national nuclear-safety report lists each reactor at 615 MWe and records the site as undergoing decommissioning, a 1,230-MWe asset retirement completed by political decision rather than construction failure.
- **State medical emergency stockpiles, and their dismantling** (Adaptability). Sweden's civil defence agency ÖCB began procuring long-term stocks of medical consumables for wartime care in 1989, and the national build-up by ÖCB and Socialstyrelsen was completed in 1995 to 1996; the stocks were run down from the late 1990s, ÖCB's stockpiles ended entirely in 2001, and by the start of the COVID-19 pandemic in 2020 Socialstyrelsen held no personal protective equipment in its emergency stockpile.
- **Botniabanan coastal railway** (Building). Sweden built the Botniabanan railway between Nyland on the Ångermanälven and Umeå through the state and municipally owned company Botniabanan AB, starting construction in 1999 and opening it in August 2010, four years after the original 2006 schedule, at a final cost of almost SEK 16 billion in 2010 prices, which the national audit office calculated as 73 percent above the original estimate.
- **Kunskapslyftet adult education initiative** (Learning). Sweden's government funded municipalities to expand upper secondary adult education from autumn 1997 until 2002, and in spring 1999 municipal adult education produced about 157,000 places, of which about 120,000 full-year places were counted within Kunskapslyftet.
- **Competence Centres Programme (Kompetenscentrumprogrammet) 1995 to 2005** (Coordination). Sweden's NUTEK, and later Vinnova and the Swedish Energy Agency, ran a ten-year programme from 1995 that built 28 competence centres at eight universities, each founded on a three-party contract between a university, a group of firms and the agency; more than 300 companies joined as contracting parties and industry put in 1.7 billion kronor between 1995 and 2005.
- **Framtidskommissionen, the Prime Minister's time-limited future commission** (Anticipation). The Swedish government appointed Framtidskommissionen, chaired by Prime Minister Fredrik Reinfeldt, in November 2011 to identify the societal challenges Sweden faces in the longer term, and by its final report in March 2013 it had held eight meetings, organised 40 open meetings, seminars and workshops, published twelve background reports and an anthology, and carried out four sub-inquiries.
